Because there's no text in it. A scanner or a phone camera saves a picture of
each page, and a PDF can hold pictures just as happily as it holds words. To a
PDF reader, a scanned contract is the same thing as a holiday photo. OCR,
optical character recognition, looks at the picture and works out which
letters are in it.

This tool does that page by page and gives you the words as plain text, to
copy or download as a .txt file.

## Check first whether you need OCR at all

Plenty of PDFs that feel like scans aren't. Anything exported from Word, a
browser or an accounting package has real text in it, and pulling that out is
exact, instant and needs no recognition at all.

The quick test: open the PDF and try to select a single word. If it
highlights, use [PDF to text](/pdf-to-txt) instead. It reads the text that's
already there, with no guessing. If your cursor draws a box over the whole
page, it's a scan, and you're in the right place.

## What you get back

Plain text, page after page, with a line marking where each page starts.
Paragraph breaks mostly survive. Formatting doesn't: no bold, no columns, no
tables, no images.

Two column layouts are the case that surprises people. Recognition reads
across the page the way the lines fall, and on a newspaper or academic paper
it can join the left and right columns into one sentence. If that happens,
crop each column into its own picture and run them through [image to
text](/image-to-text) one at a time.

You don't get a searchable PDF back, the kind with an invisible text layer
under the scan. You get the text itself.

## How accurate it is

On a clean, straight, black-on-white scan of printed text, nearly every word
comes out right. Expect the odd slip with lookalike characters: a 1 for an l,
an O for a 0, rn read as m.

Accuracy drops with each of these:

- **Angle.** A page photographed from the side, or curving into the spine of a
  book.
- **Light.** Shadows across the page, glare on glossy paper, a dim room.
- **Small print.** Footnotes and the small text on receipts.
- **Old or faded type.** Typewriter pages, carbon copies, thermal receipts that
  have gone pale.
- **Handwriting.** This isn't what it's for. Neat capitals sometimes work.
  Joined up writing mostly doesn't.

Read the result through before you rely on it, and check every number. A
wrong letter in a word is easy to spot. A wrong digit in an amount or a date
looks exactly like a right one.

## Choosing the language

Pick the language the document is written in. Recognition uses what it knows
about a language's letters and common words to settle doubtful characters, so
the wrong choice makes it worse, especially with accents. A French page read as
English loses its é and è.

There are eleven to choose from: English, French, German, Spanish, Portuguese,
Italian, Dutch, Arabic, simplified Chinese, Hindi and Russian. One language is
used per run. A document that mixes two will read best in whichever language
most of it is in.

## Getting a better result

The scan matters more than anything you do afterwards.

- Scan rather than photograph when you can. A flatbed scanner gives a straight,
  evenly lit page every time.
- If you're using a phone, hold it directly above the page, in good daylight,
  with the page filling the frame. A scanning app that straightens the page
  helps a lot.
- Choose black and white or greyscale over colour when the scanner asks. It
  makes the letters cleaner, and smaller files are quicker to read.
- Rotate pages so they're upright before you start. Sideways pages read badly.

## How long it takes

The first run downloads the recogniser and the language data, a few megabytes,
and your browser keeps them for next time. After that, the time depends on
the number of pages and how fast your device is, and an older phone is
noticeably slower than a laptop. The text appears page by page as it goes, so
you can start reading before a long document finishes.

## Where the work happens

The recognising runs in your browser. The PDF isn't uploaded anywhere: the
only thing downloaded is the recogniser itself, the first time. That makes it
reasonable to use on documents you wouldn't email to a stranger, like bank
statements, contracts and medical letters.
