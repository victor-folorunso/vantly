A 20% rise followed by a 20% cut leaves you 4% below where you started. The
rise is 20% of the old number and the cut is 20% of the new, bigger one, so the
cut takes away more than the rise added. To get back to the start after a 20%
rise you need a cut of about 16.67%.

That one surprise is behind most of the arguments people have about
percentages. The rest of this page is the three sums the calculator does, and
where each one goes wrong.

## What is X% of Y

Multiply Y by X and divide by 100. 15% of 200 is 30.

The order doesn't matter, which is a handy trick for doing it in your head: 8%
of 50 is the same as 50% of 8, which is 4. If one way round looks hard, try the
other.

The usual mistake is taking the percentage of the wrong thing. A 10% discount
on a price that already includes tax is not the same as 10% off before tax,
once the tax is added back. Before you work anything out, say out loud what
the "of" is.

## X is what percent of Y

Divide X by Y and multiply by 100. 50 is 25% of 200.

The thing to get right is which number goes on the bottom. "What share of the
class passed" puts the whole class on the bottom. "How big is the pass count
compared with the fail count" puts the fails on the bottom, and gives a
completely different answer from the same data.

A result over 100% isn't an error. 300 is 150% of 200. It only means X is
bigger than Y.

## Percentage change

Take the new number, subtract the old one, divide by the old one, multiply by
100. From 120 to 150 is a rise of 25%.

The old number always goes on the bottom, and that's where the asymmetry comes
from. Going back down from 150 to 120 divides by 150, not 120, so it comes out
as a 20% fall. Same two numbers, same gap of 30, two different percentages.
The calculator shows both directions for that reason.

Some cases that come up a lot:

| Change | To undo it you need |
|---|---|
| Up 10% | Down 9.09% |
| Up 25% | Down 20% |
| Up 50% | Down 33.33% |
| Up 100% (doubled) | Down 50% (halved) |
| Down 50% | Up 100% |

The last line is the painful one for investors. Lose half and you need to
double what's left just to break even.

## Percent and percentage points

When a rate moves from 4% to 5%, it has risen by one percentage point, and by
25%. Both are true. They answer different questions.

Points are the plain difference between two percentages. Percent is the change
relative to where it started. News reports often use whichever sounds more
dramatic, so an interest rate "rising 25%" and "rising one point" can describe
the same day. If a figure seems alarming, check which one you're being given.

## Stacked percentages don't add

Two 10% discounts are not a 20% discount. The second 10% comes off the already
reduced price, so the total is 19%. Three 10% discounts make 27.1%, not 30%.

The same goes the other way. Prices rising 5% a year for three years have risen
about 15.8% in total, not 15%. The gap is small over a few steps and large over
many, which is the whole idea behind [compound
interest](/compound-interest-calculator).

To combine percentages properly, turn each one into a multiplier and multiply
them together. A 10% rise is 1.10, a 10% cut is 0.90. A rise then a cut is
1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99, which is 1% down overall.

## Rounding

The calculator gives two decimal places. That's plenty for almost everything,
but rounded percentages don't always add up to 100. Three equal shares are
33.33% each, and three of those make 99.99%. If you're publishing a breakdown
that has to total exactly 100, round all but one, and give the last share
whatever is left.

## When the old number is zero

Percentage change from zero can't be worked out, because it means dividing by
zero. Going from 0 sales to 12 sales isn't an infinite increase, it's a start.
The calculator leaves the answer blank rather than invent a number. Report the
change as the plain difference instead.
