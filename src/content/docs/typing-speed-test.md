About 40 words a minute is average for someone comfortable with a keyboard.
Around 65 to 75 is a quick office typist. Above 90 is genuinely fast, and above
120 is rare enough that most people who claim it are quoting a burst rather
than a sustained minute.

For jobs that ask, transcription and data entry usually want 60 or more with
high accuracy. Most other roles that mention typing don't test it.

## A word is five characters

Not an actual word. Typing speed has been measured in five character units,
including the space, since long before computers, and the reason is that real
words vary wildly in length. Counting real words would mean a passage of short
words scored higher than the same effort spent on long ones.

So a minute spent typing 300 characters is 60 words a minute regardless of what
those characters were. That's what makes scores comparable between one test and
another.

If a test tells you a number wildly different from everywhere else, check
whether it's counting actual words. Some do, and those scores can't be compared
with anything.

## Net and raw

Raw speed counts every character you typed. Net speed counts only the ones that
matched. The gap between them is your accuracy, and it's the more useful number
of the two.

Somebody typing 90 raw at 85 percent accuracy is slower in practice than
somebody typing 65 at 99 percent, because every error has to be found and fixed,
and finding it costs more than the keystroke did. In real work, the correcting
is the expensive part.

If your accuracy is below about 95 percent, slowing down will make you faster.
That sounds like advice from a poster, and it's arithmetic: at 90 percent you're
making an error every ten characters, and each one costs several keystrokes and
a break in your attention.

## Why your score varies between tests

**The words.** Tests drawn from the most common few hundred English words score
higher than tests using ordinary prose, because common words are short and your
hands already know them. This one uses common words, so treat it as the
optimistic end of your range.

**Punctuation and capitals.** Adding them drops most people by 10 to 20 percent.
A test without them isn't measuring the typing you actually do.

**Length.** Fifteen seconds flatters you. Anyone can sprint. Sixty seconds is
the standard, and two minutes is where stamina starts showing.

**The keyboard.** Switching between a laptop and an external keyboard is worth
several words a minute in either direction until your hands adjust.

## Getting faster

The thing that helps most is not looking down. If you're hunting for keys, no
amount of practice at speed fixes it, because you're practising the hunting.
Covering your hands feels awful for about a week and then it doesn't.

Second is consistency over intensity. Ten minutes a day beats an hour on
Sunday, because this is motor memory and motor memory is built by repetition
spaced out over time.

Third, practise the things you get wrong rather than the things you get right.
Most people have a handful of letter pairs their fingers fumble every time. Ten
minutes on those is worth an hour of general practice.

Progress is slow and then it isn't. People typically sit at a number for weeks
and then jump, which is normal and worth expecting so you don't give up in the
flat part.
